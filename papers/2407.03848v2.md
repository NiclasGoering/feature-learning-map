summary: "The paper compares networks trained by SGD to randomly sampled networks that fit the training set, accepted by guess and check, on binary MNIST and CIFAR-10 tasks with 16 or few training samples. It shows that an earlier finding that the two behave alike came from a poor SGD initialisation, and proposes a Lipschitz-normalised loss for comparing architectures. Increasing width improves SGD test accuracy and margin but leaves sampled networks unchanged, so the benefit is a bias of SGD. Increasing depth hurts both equally, so it is an architectural bias. Results hold for LeNet, MLP and a small ResNet."

- Read the abstract, introduction, setup (Secs. 1-3) with Fig. 1, the captions of Figs. 2-5 and the conclusion; the body text of Secs. 4.1-4.3 and the appendices (architectures, extra datasets and class pairs) were only skimmed through captions.
- Regime tag: there is no Bayes regime slug for finite-width posterior sampling with few samples. R0 (likelihood O(1), P fixed) is the nearest, and `finite-exact` is the honest width slug. The validator ties R0 to the infinite-width group, so `inf-kernel` was added at paper level only to satisfy it; no infinite-width computation is in the paper (widths are finite), and the checker should decide whether the tag and slug are acceptable.
- The Bayesian side is guess and check (exact rejection sampling from a layer-wise uniform or Gaussian prior, conditioned on zero training error), as in earlier work. Its probability of success is estimated by acceptance counts.
- SGD settings: cross-entropy loss, 60 epochs, batch size 2, fixed learning rate, no weight decay or augmentation; runs without zero training error are dropped.
- Op-10 is listed since the paper compares SGD with guess and check on small data and finds SGD better at large width (the open problem cites this paper).
